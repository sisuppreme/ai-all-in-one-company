## 1. Department standard

### 1.1 Conformance baseline: WCAG 2.2 Level AA

- The department baseline is **WCAG 2.2, Level A + AA**, W3C Recommendation (first published 5 October 2023; revised Recommendation 12 December 2024). WCAG 2.2 keeps the WCAG 2.0/2.1 criteria, removes 4.1.1 Parsing as obsolete, and adds nine success criteria.
- The 2.2 additions that change build behavior at AA: **2.4.11 Focus Not Obscured (Minimum)** — a focused component must not be entirely hidden by author-created content (sticky headers, cookie banners, chat widgets); **2.5.7 Dragging Movements** — every drag action needs a single-pointer alternative; **2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum)** — pointer targets at least 24×24 CSS px or with equivalent spacing; **3.3.8 Accessible Authentication (Minimum)** — no cognitive-function test (memorized codes, puzzles, blocked paste) as the only login path. New Level A: **3.2.6 Consistent Help**, **3.3.7 Redundant Entry**.
- Why 2.2 and not the version a given law cites: laws lag the standard (see 1.2). Building to 2.2 AA is backward-compatible with 2.1/2.0 AA obligations in almost every case, because 2.2 only removes one criterion (4.1.1) that browsers now satisfy inherently. Phase 3 skills must state the target as "WCAG 2.2 AA" and note where a contract or law pins an older version.
- Conformance is per full page and per complete process (WCAG conformance requirements): a checkout is not conformant if step 3 fails, even if steps 1–2 pass. Partial conformance statements and third-party-content carve-outs exist in the spec but are not a planning tool.

### 1.2 Legal landscape (orientation only — not legal advice; obligations vary by jurisdiction, sector, and customer)

- **EU — European Accessibility Act (EAA), Directive (EU) 2019/882.** Applies from **28 June 2025** to listed products and services offered to EU consumers (e-commerce, consumer banking, electronic communications, e-books, audiovisual media access, transport ticketing/information, 112 emergency communications, plus hardware categories). Requirements in Annex I are functional (perceivable, operable, understandable, robust). Under Article 15, conformance with a cited harmonised standard gives a **presumption of conformity**; for ICT that standard is **EN 301 549**. The currently cited version, EN 301 549 V3.2.1, incorporates **WCAG 2.1 Level AA**. A V4.1.1 revision moving to WCAG 2.2 has been published (September 2026), but presumption of conformity attaches only once the Commission cites it in the Official Journal — so the legally safe EU floor today is EN 301 549 / WCAG 2.1 AA, while the department build target stays 2.2 AA. The EAA is a directive: it is enforced through **national transposition laws**, so penalties, market-surveillance bodies, and exemptions (e.g., the services microenterprise carve-out; the Article 14 disproportionate-burden defense, which must be documented) differ by member state. The older EU Web Accessibility Directive (2016/2102) separately covers public-sector bodies.
- **US federal — Section 508 of the Rehabilitation Act.** The Revised 508 Standards (Access Board final rule, January 2017; compliance required from 18 January 2018) **incorporate WCAG 2.0 Level A and AA by reference** for ICT that federal agencies develop, procure, maintain, or use, and the FAR (Subpart 39.2) extends this to what agencies buy. Consequence for vendors: a federal contract can pin you to WCAG **2.0** AA even though the department target is 2.2 — procurement documents (and the VPAT®/ACR reporting format built on them) must be read per engagement.
- **US — ADA.** The DOJ's **Title II final rule (April 2024)** sets **WCAG 2.1 Level AA** as the technical standard for web content and mobile apps of state and local governments; compliance dates are phased by entity population and were **extended by a 2026 interim final rule** (to 26 April 2027 for entities serving 50,000+, and 26 April 2028 for smaller entities and special districts, per DOJ's published extension). **Title III** (private businesses as public accommodations) has **no adopted web-specific technical standard**; DOJ guidance has pointed to WCAG as the benchmark, and private litigation over website accessibility is an established risk in the US. Net: "ADA compliant" is not a single testable claim — skills must ask *which title, which jurisdiction, which WCAG version*.
- **Elsewhere.** Other jurisdictions (e.g., Canada's ACA/AODA in Ontario, the UK Equality Act with the public-sector regulations referencing WCAG, Australia's DDA) impose their own duties, usually WCAG-referencing but not identical. The department rule: never state a legal conclusion in a deliverable; state the technical conformance result (standard, version, level, date, scope) and flag that legal applicability is for counsel/D14 to confirm.

### 1.3 ICU / i18n practice standard

- **No hard-coded user-facing strings.** All copy lives in message catalogs keyed by meaning, not by English text or screen position.
- **ICU MessageFormat** is the message syntax standard: plural/select/selectordinal logic lives inside the message, driven by **Unicode CLDR** plural rules. CLDR defines up to six plural categories (zero, one, two, few, many, other); languages use different subsets — Japanese uses one (other), English two (one/other), Polish four (one/few/many/other), Arabic all six. Code passes raw values; it never branches on `count == 1`.
- **All formatting goes through locale APIs** backed by CLDR data: `Intl.DateTimeFormat`, `Intl.NumberFormat`, `Intl.PluralRules`, `Intl.ListFormat`, `Intl.RelativeTimeFormat` (or ICU4J/ICU4C equivalents). Dates, numbers, currencies, units, and name ordering are never assembled by string concatenation. Currency display ≠ currency conversion; format the amount in its own currency unless the product explicitly converts.
- **Language negotiation and metadata:** every page/document carries a correct BCP 47 `lang` (and `dir` where applicable); language switching changes the full UI, not just body copy; locale fallback chains are explicit (e.g., `ar-EG` → `ar` → source locale), and untranslated strings fall back visibly in staging, never silently in production.
- **Pseudo-localization in CI** (expanded, accented, bracketed pseudo-locale) is the standard cheap gate for hard-coded strings, truncation, and concatenation bugs before any translator is paid.

### 1.4 RTL mirroring rules (summary; detail in the rtl-layouts skill notes)

- Set `dir="rtl"` on the document root for RTL locales; **never** infer direction from `lang` in user agents' place, and never fake mirroring with CSS transforms (`scaleX(-1)`) on layout.
- Build layout with **CSS logical properties** (`margin-inline-start`, `padding-inline`, `inset-inline-start`, `border-start-start-radius`, `text-align: start`) so one stylesheet serves both directions. Flexbox and grid follow direction automatically; physical properties (`left`, `margin-left`) are the defect.
- **Mirror:** navigation order, progress indicators, back/forward chevrons, directional flow diagrams, media-player progress, breadcrumb order. **Do not mirror:** clocks and time representations, charts/graphs axes tied to data conventions, logos and brand marks, phone keypads, music/video content itself, icons whose direction is semantic in the real world (e.g., a clock face). Play icons are a judgment call documented per design system — decide once, in the tokens, not per screen.
- **Bidi text is separate from layout mirroring:** mixed-direction strings (an English product name inside an Arabic sentence, numbers inside Hebrew text) are governed by the Unicode Bidirectional Algorithm; isolate embedded runs with `dir` on inline elements or `<bdi>`/unicode isolates, or punctuation and numbers land on the wrong side (W3C i18n technique H56 and the W3C bidi guidance).
- Numbers: decide per locale whether Western (Latin) or locale-native digits are expected (CLDR numbering systems); dates in RTL locales still follow the locale calendar/format via `Intl`, not hand-reversed strings.

## 2. Failure modes this department prevents

From PROJECT_ANATOMY.md Part 6, "Accessibility" element (slop lines):

- Alt text missing or filled with file names.
- Placeholder-only form labels (no persistent, programmatic label).
- Focus outlines removed for looks; dialogs that trap or lose focus.
- Click targets too small (below the 2.5.8 floor).
- An "accessibility overlay" widget claimed as the fix instead of fixing the code.
- Clickable `<div>`s instead of buttons — controls with no role, name, or keyboard path.

From "Internationalisation and RTL" element (slop lines):

- English strings embedded in components; dates formatted by string concatenation.
- Layouts built on physical left/right properties that collapse under `dir="rtl"` — an LTR page with Arabic words pasted in.
- Plural rules broken by `count == 1` branching; strings overflowing containers when translated.
- Machine-translated text shipped without human review.

Adjacent Part 6/7 slop this department co-owns: error text conveyed by color alone, captions absent on media, motion that ignores `prefers-reduced-motion` (Part 7 motion element), and form errors announced visually but not to assistive technology.

## 3. Per-skill research notes

### 3.1 accessibility-audit

- **Core method:** A scoped, evidence-producing evaluation, not a scan. Define scope (pages, flows, components, WCAG version/level), run the automated pass (axe-core), then manual passes: keyboard walk, screen-reader smoke test, visual checks (zoom 200%/400% reflow, contrast measurement, focus visibility). Record every finding against a success-criterion number with reproduction steps and a screenshot/recording. Severity ranks by user impact on task completion, not by tool output order. Re-audit after fixes; an audit report is dated and versioned because conformance decays with every release.
- **Key rules:**
  1. Automated tools evaluate only a subset of criteria; an audit that is only a tool run is incomplete and must say so in its own report.
  2. Every finding cites the WCAG success criterion it fails (e.g., 1.4.3), never just "accessibility issue."
  3. Audit complete processes (register → verify → first task), not isolated pages.
  4. Include at least one real screen reader and one keyboard-only pass in every audit scope.
  5. Report conformance as "supports / partially supports / does not support" per criterion (VPAT-style) when the output feeds procurement.
- **Verification gates:** axe-core run over the audit scope with the report archived (automatable); audit checklist artifact exists with each in-scope criterion marked pass/fail/not-applicable; findings register with zero un-triaged criticals before sign-off.
- **Sources:** WCAG 2.2, W3C — https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/ ; axe-core, Deque — https://github.com/dequelabs/axe-core

### 3.2 wcag

- **Core method:** The conformance engine of the department. Work criterion-by-criterion from the normative text, using W3C's "Understanding" documents for intent and documented techniques/failures for implementation. Apply the five conformance requirements (full pages, complete processes, accessibility-supported technology use, non-interference). For each project, fix the target (version + level) in writing before build, and map design tokens (contrast pairs, target sizes, focus styles) to the criteria they satisfy so most conformance is inherited from the design system.
- **Key rules:**
  1. Contrast floors: 4.5:1 for normal text, 3:1 for large text and for non-text UI components/graphics (1.4.3, 1.4.11) — measured on the actual rendered colors, including states.
  2. Focus must be visible (2.4.7) and not entirely obscured (2.4.11); sticky headers/banners are the default failure.
  3. Targets ≥24×24 CSS px or spacing-equivalent (2.5.8); design target stays larger (44px-class) where space allows.
  4. No keyboard traps (2.1.2); everything operable by keyboard alone (2.1.1) with a logical focus order (2.4.3).
  5. Name, role, value must be programmatically determinable for every component (4.1.2); status messages announced without focus moves (4.1.3).
- **Verification gates:** contrast pairs in the token file checked by script against 1.4.3/1.4.11 ratios (automatable); axe-core rules mapped to criteria pass in CI; per-release criterion checklist for changed screens.
- **Sources:** WCAG 2.2, W3C — https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/ ; "What's New in WCAG 2.2", W3C WAI — https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/new-in-22/

### 3.3 screen-readers

- **Core method:** Test the top user journeys in the screen readers users actually pair with each platform: NVDA + Chrome/Firefox and JAWS + Chrome on Windows, VoiceOver + Safari on macOS and iOS, TalkBack + Chrome on Android. Navigate by headings, landmarks, links, and form controls (the way screen-reader users actually move), not only by reading order. Verify accessible names, roles, states, table headers, live-region announcements, and that dynamic changes (toasts, step changes, errors) are announced without stealing focus. Fix semantics in HTML first; ARIA only where HTML has no native element, following the rule that no ARIA is better than bad ARIA.
- **Key rules:**
  1. Every interactive element exposes an accessible name that matches or contains its visible label (2.5.3 Label in Name — also what voice-control users depend on).
  2. Heading structure is a navigation outline, not font sizing; landmarks are few and labeled when repeated.
  3. Images: informative images get real alt text; decorative images get empty `alt=""` — never a file name.
  4. Custom widgets follow the ARIA Authoring Practices patterns (keyboard model + roles/states) or are replaced with native elements.
  5. Live regions are polite by default; assertive is reserved for errors that block the task.
- **Verification gates:** scripted journey checklist executed in at least one desktop (NVDA) and one mobile (VoiceOver or TalkBack) screen reader per release, with pass/fail recorded (manual gate — cannot be automated); automated name/role checks via axe-core in CI (partially automatable).
- **Sources:** ARIA Authoring Practices Guide (APG), W3C — https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/ ; WCAG 2.2 — https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/

### 3.4 accessibility-law

- **Core method:** A jurisdiction-mapping exercise that produces a *technical target*, not legal opinions. For each engagement: identify where users are, what sector the product is in, and who the customer is (public procurement changes everything); map those to the instruments in §1.2 (EAA/EN 301 549, Section 508, ADA Title II/III, WAD, national laws); record the pinned WCAG version and level, any required conformance artifact (accessibility statement under the WAD/EAA model, ACR/VPAT for US procurement), and the review date. Escalate interpretation questions to D14/counsel. Re-check on scope change (new market, new sector, government customer).
- **Key rules:**
  1. Never write "ADA compliant" or "legally accessible" in a deliverable; write the technical result: standard, version, level, scope, date.
  2. The law that applies follows the user's/customer's jurisdiction, not the developer's.
  3. Where versions conflict (508 pins WCAG 2.0; EAA's cited EN 301 549 pins 2.1; department target is 2.2), build to the newest and document conformance against the pinned version.
  4. Accessibility statements, where required, must include the conformance status, known exceptions, and a feedback/contact mechanism — and must be true.
  5. Exemptions and defenses (disproportionate burden, fundamental alteration, microenterprise carve-outs) are documented legal determinations, never engineering assumptions.
- **Verification gates:** jurisdiction map artifact exists in the project file set before launch, with pinned standard/version and a named reviewer (manual gate); accessibility statement content checked against the audit result before publication (automatable diff of claimed vs. audited scope).
- **Sources:** Directive (EU) 2019/882 summary, EUR-Lex — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=legissum%3A4403933 ; Revised 508 Standards, US Access Board — https://www.access-board.gov/ict/

### 3.5 keyboard-accessibility

- **Core method:** Every function reachable and operable with keyboard alone, in a visible, logical order. Use native interactive elements first (they bring focusability, key handling, and roles for free). For custom widgets, implement the APG keyboard model exactly (arrow keys inside composites like tabs/menus/grids, Tab moves between components, Esc closes dialogs/menus). Manage focus deliberately: dialogs trap focus while open and return it to the invoker on close; route changes and dynamic insertions move focus only when the user would otherwise be stranded. Never remove the focus outline without an equal-or-better replacement.
- **Key rules:**
  1. No keyboard trap anywhere, including embedded third-party widgets (2.1.2).
  2. Focus order follows the visual/logical reading order; positive `tabindex` values are banned (2.4.3).
  3. Focus indicator visible on every interactive element in every state (2.4.7), and not fully hidden by sticky chrome (2.4.11) — use `scroll-margin` where sticky headers exist.
  4. Skip links (or an equivalent bypass) on pages with repeated navigation (2.4.1).
  5. Drag interactions, sliders, and sortable lists have full keyboard equivalents (2.5.7's keyboard counterpart; APG patterns).
- **Verification gates:** scripted keyboard walk of the primary flows — Tab/Shift+Tab/Enter/Space/arrows only — recorded as a pass/fail checklist in CI-adjacent QA (semi-automatable with Playwright key scripts plus visual assertion); lint rule banning `tabindex > 0` and `outline: none` without replacement (automatable).
- **Sources:** ARIA Authoring Practices Guide, W3C — https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/ ; WCAG 2.2 criteria 2.1.1–2.4.13 — https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/

### 3.6 reduced-motion

- **Core method:** Respect the operating system's `prefers-reduced-motion` setting as a user instruction, not a preference hint. Inventory all motion in the product (scroll animations, parallax, carousels, auto-playing media, page transitions, animated counters) and give each a reduced-motion behavior: remove, replace with an instant change or crossfade, or gate behind explicit user activation. Separately meet the normative floors: nothing flashes more than three times per second (2.3.1), moving/auto-updating content can be paused, stopped, or hidden (2.2.2), and non-essential animation triggered by interaction can be disabled (2.3.3, AAA — adopted here as department practice even though the baseline is AA).
- **Key rules:**
  1. A global `@media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce)` block neutralizes decorative animation and smooth scrolling; components do not opt out individually.
  2. Auto-playing video/carousels always ship with a visible pause control, in every motion mode.
  3. Essential motion (e.g., a progress indicator) may remain but must not involve vestibular-risk patterns: no parallax, no zoom/pan of large screen areas, no spinning.
  4. Animation must never be the only carrier of meaning (state changes also change text, icon, or ARIA state).
  5. New motion in a design review is justified like new copy: what it communicates, and its reduced-motion equivalent, in the same ticket.
- **Verification gates:** emulate `prefers-reduced-motion: reduce` in the browser/Playwright and walk the primary flows, confirming no non-essential animation runs (automatable via Playwright's `reducedMotion` emulation plus a CSS audit for un-gated `@keyframes`/`transition` on animated components); flash check on video content before publication (manual/tool-assisted).
- **Sources:** WCAG 2.2 criteria 2.2.2, 2.3.1, 2.3.3 — https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/ ; `prefers-reduced-motion`, MDN — https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/@media/prefers-reduced-motion

### 3.7 internationalization

- **Core method:** Engineer the source product so localization is data, not code changes. Extract every user-facing string (UI, emails, notifications, error messages, alt text, PDF templates) into message catalogs keyed by meaning. Use ICU MessageFormat for any string with plurals, gender, lists, or embedded numbers/dates, and route all formatting through `Intl`/ICU with CLDR data. Design for text expansion and contraction: flexible containers, no fixed-width text buttons, no text baked into images. Set `lang` (BCP 47) on every document and on in-line language changes — which is also a WCAG requirement (3.1.1, 3.1.2) and how screen readers pick pronunciation.
- **Key rules:**
  1. No string concatenation for sentences; word order is locale property, so whole messages are translated as units.
  2. Plurals come from CLDR categories via MessageFormat/`Intl.PluralRules` — code never branches on count.
  3. Dates, times, numbers, currencies, units, and relative time ("in 3 days") always via locale APIs with explicit time zone handling.
  4. Sort order, calendars, first-day-of-week, and name/address formats are locale data, not constants.
  5. Every string has a context note for translators (where it appears, character limit, placeholders explained) at authoring time.
- **Verification gates:** codebase grep/lint for user-facing literals outside catalogs returns zero (automatable); pseudo-localization build renders all primary screens without truncation or mojibake (automatable screenshot pass); `Intl` usage audit — no hand-rolled date/number formatting functions (automatable lint).
- **Sources:** ICU, Unicode — https://icu.unicode.org/home ; Unicode CLDR plural rules — https://cldr.unicode.org/index/cldr-spec/plural-rules

### 3.8 localization-workflow

- **Core method:** A pipeline with states, not an email with a spreadsheet. Source strings freeze per release; catalogs (with context notes and screenshots) go to translators in a translation management system; translations return through automated checks (placeholder integrity, ICU syntax validity, missing keys, length) before human review; in-context review happens on a staging build in the real UI; native-speaker review is mandatory for legal, payment, health, and safety strings. Machine translation may draft low-risk strings but never ships unreviewed — the anatomy slop line is explicit. Fallback behavior is defined per string class: untranslated UI falls back to the source locale visibly in staging and is a release blocker for primary flows.
- **Key rules:**
  1. Placeholder and ICU-syntax integrity is machine-checked on every import; a broken placeholder is a build failure, not a typo.
  2. Translation memory and a project glossary (product terms, do-not-translate list) are maintained so terms stay consistent across releases.
  3. Character/byte limits and expansion risk are flagged at translation time, not discovered in layout QA.
  4. Every locale has a named reviewer; "the vendor delivered it" is not review.
  5. String changes after freeze go through change control — a one-word source change invalidates the translation of that key in every locale until re-checked.
- **Verification gates:** TMS/CI validation report per release: zero missing keys in primary locales, zero placeholder mismatches, zero invalid ICU messages (automatable); in-context review sign-off recorded per locale for primary flows (manual gate).
- **Sources:** ICU MessageFormat documentation, Unicode — https://unicode-org.github.io/icu/userguide/format_parse/messages/ ; CLDR, Unicode — https://cldr.unicode.org/

### 3.9 rtl-layouts

- **Core method:** Flip the document, not the stylesheet. Serve RTL locales with `dir="rtl"` at the root and a layout built entirely on CSS logical properties and direction-aware flex/grid, so mirroring is automatic. Then do a visual pass per screen class for the things automation misses: icons that must or must not flip, embedded LTR content (code, URLs, phone numbers) that must stay LTR and be isolated, charts and media controls, and text truncation direction. Test with real Arabic (and Urdu where targeted — Nastaliq script has very different line-height behavior from Arabic Naskh, so line-height and clipping must be checked per script, not assumed from Arabic).
- **Key rules:**
  1. Physical directional CSS (`left`, `right`, `margin-left`, `float: left`) in component code is a defect; logical properties or direction-agnostic layout only (stylelint-enforced).
  2. Directional icons (back, forward, send, reply) flip; semantic icons (clock, play on media, logos) do not — the flip list lives in the design system, decided once.
  3. Embedded opposite-direction runs are isolated (`<bdi>`, `dir="auto"` where appropriate, unicode isolates) so numbers and punctuation order correctly (Unicode Bidi Algorithm).
  4. Scrollbars, carousels, sliders, and progress indicators follow the inline direction; horizontal scroll regions start from inline-start.
  5. Never mirror with transforms on containers; never ship an RTL locale whose charts/maps were not individually reviewed.
- **Verification gates:** stylelint rule banning physical directional properties in new code (automatable); screenshot comparison of key screens in LTR vs. RTL locale in CI with human review of the diff set (semi-automatable); bidi test strings (mixed Arabic/English/numbers) rendered and checked on data-dense screens (manual checklist).
- **Sources:** "Internationalization Best Practices: Handling Right-to-left Scripts in HTML", W3C — https://www.w3.org/International/geo/html-tech/tech-bidi.html ; CSS Logical Properties and Values, W3C (via MDN) — https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/CSS_logical_properties_and_values

### 3.10 inclusive-copy

- **Core method:** Write interface copy that the widest range of users can understand and act on: plain language at a lower-secondary reading level for task copy, front-loaded instructions (what to do before why), errors that say what happened and how to fix it in the user's terms, and consistent terminology (one name per object, everywhere, including translations). Avoid idioms, slang, culture-specific metaphors, and gendered or ability-assuming language ("simply click", "blind to", "crazy fast"); where a language requires gendered forms, MessageFormat `select` handles it deliberately rather than by accident. Alt text, link text, and button labels are copy: they describe purpose/destination, never "click here" or the file name.
- **Key rules:**
  1. Link and button text makes sense out of context (screen-reader users navigate by link lists) — and matches the accessible-name rules in §3.3.
  2. Error messages: name the field, state the problem, give the fix; never color-only, never blame ("you entered an invalid…"), never a bare code.
  3. Instructions appear before the control they describe, and required-format hints (password rules, date formats) are given before entry, not after failure (WCAG 3.3.1–3.3.3 practice).
  4. Reading level and terminology are checked against the project glossary; the same concept never appears under two names in one product.
  5. Copy is reviewed in translation for idiom and formality level (e.g., German Sie/du, Spanish tú/usted) per locale style guide — politeness is a locale decision, not a default.
- **Verification gates:** lint pass over catalogs for banned patterns (placeholder-only labels, "click here", color-only error references) (automatable); human plain-language review of primary-flow copy and all error strings before release (manual gate); glossary conformance check in the TMS (automatable).
- **Sources:** WCAG 2.2 criteria 2.4.4, 2.5.3, 3.3.1–3.3.3 — https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/ ; W3C WAI, "Writing for Web Accessibility" — https://www.w3.org/WAI/tips/writing/

## 4. Open risks for Phase 3

- **Automation ceiling.** axe-core and lint gates catch only the mechanically testable subset of criteria. They cannot judge whether alt text is *correct*, whether focus order is *sensible*, whether a live region announcement is *understandable*, or whether an error message actually helps. Phase 3 skills must pair every automated gate with a named manual gate, or the department will produce green dashboards over unusable products.
- **Screen-reader quality is a matrix problem.** NVDA/JAWS/VoiceOver/TalkBack × browser × version combinations behave differently on the same markup, especially for custom widgets and live regions. Skills must fix a small, explicit test matrix (and a "last tested" date per journey) instead of claiming general screen-reader support; untested combinations remain a stated residual risk.
- **Cognitive accessibility is under-specified by WCAG AA.** Plain language, consistent help, redundant-entry, and accessible authentication (the 2.2 additions) only partly cover users with cognitive and learning disabilities; much of the deeper guidance sits at AAA or in non-normative W3C work (e.g., COGA "Making Content Usable"). Phase 3 must decide explicitly which above-AA practices (reading level targets, timeout warnings, undo for destructive actions) are department standard, rather than implying AA covers it.
- **Legal drift.** The legal floor moves: EN 301 549 V4.1.1 (WCAG 2.2) is published but presumption of conformity depends on Official Journal citation; ADA Title II dates were already extended once by interim rule; national EAA enforcement practice is still forming. accessibility-law notes must carry review dates, and Phase 3 should schedule re-verification rather than freezing 2026 facts into skills.
- **RTL beyond Arabic.** Arabic passing does not imply Urdu (Nastaliq line-height/clipping), Persian, or Hebrew (bidi with numbers/Latin brands) pass. Per-script visual QA is a cost Phase 3 workflows must budget, and pseudo-RTL testing catches layout flips but not script-shaping defects.
- **Third-party components.** Payment widgets, chat embeds, cookie banners, and video players routinely break focus order, obscure focus (2.4.11), or ship inaccessible iframes outside the team's code control. Skills need a vendor-evaluation step and contractual ACR requirements; "the vendor's widget" is not a conformance exemption.
- **Translation quality vs. launch pressure.** The workflow gates (native review, in-context sign-off) are the first thing cut under deadline pressure. Phase 3 should define the non-negotiable subset (legal, payment, health, safety, primary-flow strings) that blocks release, so the trade-off is explicit rather than silent.
- **Locale scope creep.** Each added locale multiplies QA surface (screen-reader language/pronunciation, formality conventions, legal statements). Skills should require a per-locale support tier (full / UI-only / best-effort) recorded in the project files, so "supports 12 languages" is a defined claim, not marketing copy.
