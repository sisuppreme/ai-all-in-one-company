**Framing rule for every D14 skill (non-negotiable):** the skill provides *implementation guidance, not legal advice*. Every legal assertion a skill makes is date-stamped and jurisdiction-tagged; where a decision exceeds implementation (novel processing, children's products, health/finance data, a contested rights call), the skill's job is to stop and require qualified counsel, not to decide. Laws, regulator guidance, adequacy lists, and member-state ages all change — a skill states the rule as of its writing date and names its source, never as eternal fact.

## 1. Department standard

### 1.1 Obligations are translated into engineering requirements, per market and per feature

Professional compliance work for web products runs as a pipeline: (1) inventory the markets served and the features shipped; (2) derive the obligation set for each market × feature combination (privacy, consent, children, payments, UGC, cloning) into a written **obligations register**; (3) translate each obligation into a product requirement with an owner and a test; (4) implement as real flows — consent capture, DSAR lifecycle, age gates, audit trails — not as policy prose; (5) re-run the derivation whenever a feature, market, or processor changes. The register is data (tables/config), not an essay, so coverage can be checked mechanically. The anatomy states the same shape: obligations "identified per market and feature… translated into product requirements, implemented as real flows… and reviewed when features or markets change."

### 1.2 The GDPR is the strictest common baseline; its clocks and records are the department's defaults

- **Principles and accountability.** Article 5 sets the principles (lawfulness/fairness/transparency, purpose limitation, minimisation, accuracy, storage limitation, integrity/confidentiality); Article 5(2) and Article 24 make the controller *able to demonstrate* compliance — which is why this department is record-driven: if it isn't logged, it didn't happen.
- **Record of processing activities (RoPA).** Article 30 requires a written record: controller identity, purposes, data categories, recipients, third-country transfers, retention, security measures. The Article 30(5) carve-out (under 250 employees) rarely applies in practice because it fails whenever processing is non-occasional, risky, or involves special-category data. ICO accountability guidance treats the RoPA as founded on a live data-mapping exercise, reviewed regularly, with a named owner.
- **Notices.** Articles 13/14 enumerate what must be told to data subjects (identity, purposes, lawful basis, recipients, transfers, retention, rights); Article 12 requires it concise, transparent, intelligible, plain-language.
- **Rights and the DSAR clock.** Articles 15–22 (access, rectification, erasure, restriction, portability, objection, automated-decision safeguards). Article 12(3): respond without undue delay and **within one month**, extendable by **two further months** for complex/numerous requests, with the extension notice given *within* the first month; Article 12(4): a refusal must state reasons and the right to complain to a supervisory authority, also within one month. Requests are free of charge unless manifestly unfounded/excessive (Article 12(5)).
- **Consent.** Article 4(11) definition (freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous); Article 7 conditions — including Article 7(1): the controller must be able to *demonstrate* consent, and Article 7(3): withdrawal must be as easy as giving.
- **DPIAs and roles.** Article 35 impact assessments for high-risk processing; Articles 37–39 DPO where triggered; Article 28 processor contracts. These are triggers the privacy-laws skill must test for, not assume away.
- Source: Regulation (EU) 2016/679, official text — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng/ ; European Commission, "Dealing with requests from individuals" — https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/data-protection/information-business-and-organisations/dealing-requests-individuals_en ; ICO accountability/RoPA audit guidance — https://cy.ico.org.uk/for-organisations/advice-and-services/audits/data-protection-audit-framework/toolkits/accountability/records-of-processing-and-lawful-basis/

### 1.3 The US is a patchwork: CCPA/CPRA is the reference implementation, not a national law

- **CCPA (as amended by CPRA), California.** Rights to know, delete, correct, opt out of sale/sharing, limit sensitive-PI use, non-discrimination. The CPPA regulations set the operating clocks: confirm receipt of a request to know/delete/correct within **10 business days**; respond within **45 calendar days**, extendable once by 45 (90 total) with notice and explanation; businesses must offer at least two submission methods and may not force account creation to submit a deletion request (account holders may be routed through the account). The regulations also require businesses to maintain records of consumer requests (regulations §7101 — records retained at least 24 months) and to honor opt-out preference signals. Applicability is threshold-based (revenue/volume/derive-revenue tests) — the skill records *whether* the product is in scope as a register entry, not an assumption.
- Other US states have their own comprehensive laws with different rights, clocks, and thresholds; there is no US federal omnibus privacy law. Variance is the rule: the department ships a per-jurisdiction table format and a worked GDPR + CCPA profile, and marks every other jurisdiction as "register before serving."
- Sources: CPPA, CCPA Regulations final text — https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/pdf/20230329_final_regs_text.pdf ; California Attorney General, CCPA page — https://oag.ca.gov/privacy/ccpa

### 1.4 Consent practice: prior, granular, symmetric, recorded

- **Cookies/trackers (EU/UK).** ePrivacy Directive Article 5(3) requires consent for non-essential storage/access on the user's device ("strictly necessary" is the only built-in exemption). The CJEU in *Planet49* (C-673/17) held a **pre-ticked checkbox is not valid consent**, and that the GDPR consent standard applies to cookies whether or not personal data is involved. ICO PECR guidance: consent must be a positive action *before* non-essential cookies are set; silence, inactivity, or "by continuing to browse" is not consent; refusing must be as easy as accepting; withdrawal must be as easy as giving; new purposes or new third parties require fresh consent. Cookie *walls* and reject-buried-in-settings designs fail the "freely given / as easy to refuse" test as regulators apply it.
- **Consent records.** A consent event is stored as data: subject, timestamp, policy/banner version, scopes granted, capture mechanism. Without the record, Article 7(1) demonstrability fails even if the banner was perfect.
- Sources: ICO, "Cookies and similar technologies" (PECR guide) — https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guide-to-pecr/cookies-and-similar-technologies/ ; *Planet49* C-673/17 (CJEU, 1 Oct 2019), EUR-Lex case summary.

### 1.5 Children: two regimes, two thresholds, per-country configuration

- **EU/UK.** GDPR Article 8: where consent is the lawful basis for an information-society service offered directly to a child, consent is valid at **16** by default; member states may lower it, **not below 13**. Verified examples of the spread: Germany 16, France 15, Spain 14, Belgium and Sweden 13; the UK sets 13 (Data Protection Act 2018, s.9). Article 8(2) requires "reasonable efforts" to verify parental authorisation. Consequence: the age threshold is **per-market configuration data**, never a hardcoded constant.
- **US.** COPPA (15 U.S.C. §§ 6501–6506; FTC Rule 16 CFR Part 312) covers operators of child-directed services and general-audience operators with actual knowledge, for children **under 13**: clear notice, direct notice to parents, **verifiable parental consent** before collection/use/disclosure (FTC-accepted methods include signed form, payment-card transaction with notice, trained-staff call/video, government-ID check with prompt deletion, knowledge-based authentication), parental review/deletion rights, data minimisation and retention limits. The FTC's amended Rule (finalised January 2025; compliance required from **22 April 2026**) adds separate parental consent for disclosures to third parties unless integral to the service, a written information-security program, a written retention policy, and biometric identifiers as personal information.
- **Design codes sit beside the statutes.** The ICO's Age Appropriate Design Code ("Children's Code") imposes design standards on services *likely to be accessed* by children in the UK — broader than a consent age. Skills treat it as a design-review input, flagged as UK-specific.
- Sources: GDPR Article 8 (EUR-Lex text above); FTC, Children's Privacy (COPPA business guidance) — https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/privacy-security/childrens-privacy

### 1.6 Open-source license hygiene: inventory, SPDX identifiers, tiered policy, shipped notices

- Every shipped component — libraries, fonts, images, copied snippets — is inventoried with its license expressed as an **SPDX License List identifier** (e.g. `MIT`, `Apache-2.0`, `GPL-3.0-only`), so policy is machine-checkable instead of name-matching prose.
- Policy is **three tiers**: allowed (permissive: MIT/BSD/ISC/Apache-2.0), review (weak copyleft and file-level copyleft: MPL-2.0, LGPL, EPL — a named human decides, in writing), forbidden without a written exception (strong/network copyleft in a distribution model it infects — AGPL-3.0 in a hosted service is the canonical case; SSPL; non-commercial terms; **no license found**, because default copyright means all rights reserved). The tier of a copyleft license depends on how the product ships (hosted vs. distributed binary), so the policy is written per distribution model.
- Scanning is source-level where it matters: package metadata misses vendored code and snippets; **ScanCode Toolkit** (AboutCode) reads files for license/copyright evidence; metadata-level checkers (`license-checker`, `pip-licenses`, `cargo-deny`) are the cheap CI layer. The inventory is emitted as an **SBOM** (SPDX or CycloneDX format) per release, and attribution obligations are discharged by a generated notices file shipped with the product. **OpenChain ISO/IEC 5230** is the reference standard for what a license-compliance program contains.
- The project's own code gets a deliberately chosen LICENSE (Choose a License is the selection aid); no LICENSE file means nobody — including future collaborators — has permission to use the code.
- Sources: SPDX License List — https://spdx.org/licenses/ ; Choose a License — https://choosealicense.com/

### 1.7 Transfers and residency: Chapter V is a mechanism test, not a hosting slogan

GDPR does not, by itself, mandate EU hosting. It restricts **transfers** to third countries (Chapter V): first check an Article 45 **adequacy decision**; failing that, Article 46 **appropriate safeguards** — in practice the Commission's 2021 **Standard Contractual Clauses** (Implementing Decision (EU) 2021/914), with the *Schrems II* duty to assess the destination's law and add supplementary measures where needed; Article 49 derogations are last-resort and narrow. Separately, some sectors and some non-EU regimes do impose hard localisation — those are register entries per market/sector, verified case by case, never generalised. The engineering artifact is a **transfer register**: every processor/sub-processor, its processing locations, and the mechanism covering each flow.
Sources: Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2021/914 (SCCs) — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32021D0914 ; Irish DPC, "Transfers of personal data to third countries" — https://www.dataprotection.ie/en/organisations/international-transfers/transfers-personal-data-third-countries-or-international-organisations

### 1.8 Audit culture: frameworks are not statutes

Compliance is demonstrated by tested controls and dated evidence (the compliance-audit skill), not by badges. **SOC 2 and ISO/IEC 27001 are voluntary trust frameworks, not laws**; claiming "GDPR compliant" or "PCI certified" in marketing without a supporting process is itself a failure mode (anatomy, Legal and compliance element). The department rule: public claims are limited to what an audit artifact can prove, worded as what was tested, when, and against what.

## 2. Failure modes this department prevents

From PROJECT_ANATOMY.md Part 6, "Privacy" element (slop lines):

- A privacy policy copied from another product naming data the app does not collect — and missing what it does.
- A cookie banner with no working reject path.
- "Delete account" that only hides the profile (erasure that does not erase).

From "Legal and compliance" element (slop lines):

- Legal pages generated from a generic template with another company's name left in.
- "By using this site you agree" with no record of agreement (no version, no timestamp, no acceptance event).
- Compliance claimed in marketing ("GDPR compliant," "PCI certified") that no process supports.

From "Licensing" element (slop lines):

- No LICENSE file at all.
- A license copied from a tutorial that names the wrong project.
- Vendored code or fonts dropped in with their license files deleted.

From Part 10, "Clone ethics" element (slop lines):

- Cloning a live brand end to end — logo, copy, and photography kept — and calling it a template.
- Scraping behind a login with borrowed credentials.
- Treating a missing robots.txt Disallow as permission (RFC 9309: robots rules "are not a form of access authorization").

From Part 10, "Asset harvesting" element (slop lines), co-owned with D16:

- Bulk-downloading a competitor's photo library and shipping it unchanged; assets entering a rebuild with no rights record.

## 3. Per-skill research notes

### 3.1 privacy-laws

**Core method.** Build the obligations register before features: enumerate markets served × data categories processed × user types, and for each cell record the governing regime, the obligations it creates (notice content, lawful basis per purpose, rights supported, retention, breach-notification duty and its clock), and the product requirement that satisfies each. Lawful basis (GDPR Article 6) is assigned per processing *purpose*, not per product. DPIA triggers (Article 35: systematic monitoring, large-scale special-category data, new technology with high risk) and DPO/representative triggers are tested explicitly and the outcome recorded, including "not triggered, because…". The register is a versioned table in the repo; a market or feature with no register row does not ship.
**Key rules.**
- No processing purpose exists without a recorded lawful basis (or the non-GDPR regime's equivalent permission) before build.
- The register is data, not prose: market, regime, obligation, requirement, owner, last-verified date — so coverage is mechanically checkable.
- Minimisation is enforced at the form and schema level: a field with no purpose in the register is removed, not policy-washed.
- Variance is stated, never averaged: where regimes conflict (e.g. retention duties vs. erasure rights), the conflict is logged and escalated to counsel, not resolved by picking the convenient rule.
- Every legal statement in the skill output is date-stamped and sourced; stale entries (past their review date) are treated as unverified.
**Gates.**
- *Automatable:* register coverage check — every market in product config and every processing purpose in the RoPA has a register row with a non-empty lawful-basis field; CI fails on a new analytics/processor SDK with no corresponding row.
- *Automatable:* review-date lint — any register row older than its review interval fails the compliance-audit checklist until re-verified.
**Sources.** GDPR, Regulation (EU) 2016/679 — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng/ ; ICO accountability framework (RoPA and governance) — https://cy.ico.org.uk/for-organisations/advice-and-services/audits/data-protection-audit-framework/toolkits/accountability/records-of-processing-and-lawful-basis/

### 3.2 data-residency

**Core method.** Maintain a transfer register derived from the data inventory and the processor list: for each processor/sub-processor, record what personal data it receives, where it is processed and stored, and the Chapter V mechanism covering the flow (adequacy / SCCs / BCR / derogation / not-a-transfer because EEA-only). Apply the hierarchy in order — adequacy first, SCCs (2021 modules matched to the transfer scenario) second with a documented transfer impact assessment post-*Schrems II*, derogations never as a standing architecture. Region choices in infrastructure config (cloud regions, CDN behaviour, support-access locations) must match the register; support access from a third country is a transfer too. Hard localisation duties (sector rules, non-EU regimes) are separate register rows, verified per market.
**Key rules.**
- No processor is integrated before its register row exists with a named transfer mechanism; "the vendor is GDPR compliant" is not a mechanism.
- Residency claims in product copy ("data stays in the EU") must be provable from the register and infra config, or deleted.
- SCC module selection and the impact assessment are documents on file, not vendor-checkbox assertions; the vendor's signed DPA/SCCs are stored with the register.
- Onward transfers count: a processor's sub-processor list is reviewed on the vendor's change notices, and register rows are updated then, not annually.
- Derogations (Article 49) are per-transfer and documented; a product flow that *depends* on a derogation is a defect.
**Gates.**
- *Automatable:* config-vs-register scan — cloud region settings in IaC/deployment config are compared against the register's approved locations; an unlisted region fails deployment review.
- *Automatable:* processor completeness check — every third-party SDK/endpoint in the codebase or network manifest has a transfer-register row with a non-empty mechanism field.
**Sources.** Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2021/914 (2021 SCCs) — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32021D0914 ; Irish DPC transfer guidance — https://www.dataprotection.ie/en/organisations/international-transfers/transfers-personal-data-third-countries-or-international-organisations

### 3.3 dsar-handling

**Core method.** Implement the request lifecycle as an explicit state machine: intake (at least two channels; no account creation forced for a deletion request beyond what the applicable regime permits) → proportionate identity verification → clock start recorded at receipt → search across primary stores, processors, and exports → exemption/redaction review → delivery in a usable format → closure record. Clocks are regime-configured: GDPR one month, +2 extension with notice inside month one (Article 12(3)); CCPA confirm receipt within 10 business days, respond in 45 calendar days, +45 with notice; other regimes entered in the same table after verification. Erasure executes against the database, search indexes, analytics, and processors, and follows the documented backups policy (deletion on rotation, access-suppressed meanwhile) — it never merely hides the profile. Refusals state reasons and the complaint route (Article 12(4)). Every request and its handling are logged; CCPA regulations require request records be kept at least 24 months.
**Key rules.**
- The clock is system-enforced (timestamps, aging alerts at 50% and 80% of deadline), never a human's calendar memory.
- Verification is proportionate: never collect *more* personal data to verify than the request itself exposes the requester to; an unverifiable request is denied with reasons, not silently dropped.
- Erasure is verified by absence: after execution, a residue query across stores and admin views must return nothing for the test subject.
- Processors are instructed as part of the flow (GDPR Article 28 contract terms; CCPA: direct service providers/contractors to delete), and their confirmations are filed with the request record.
- The intake path itself is tested end-to-end on a schedule — a broken request form is a compliance failure, not a UX bug.
**Gates.**
- *Automatable:* DSAR drill — a test access request and a test erasure request are submitted through the real product flow; the gate passes only if the export is complete against the test subject's known data and post-erasure residue queries return zero across DB and admin views (the anatomy's own verify step).
- *Automatable:* clock dashboard — open requests carry computed deadlines per regime; any request past 80% of its clock without a recorded extension notice raises an alert.
**Sources.** GDPR Articles 12–22 (EUR-Lex text) — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng/ ; CPPA, CCPA Regulations (response clocks, request records) — https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/pdf/20230329_final_regs_text.pdf

### 3.4 legal-documents

**Core method.** Terms, privacy notice, and cookie policy are generated *from the product's own artifacts* — the data inventory/RoPA, processor register, and feature list — never from a generic template. Each document is versioned; publication stores the full text of every version; acceptance is captured as an event (user, document version, timestamp, mechanism) at signup and on material change, with a re-acceptance flow for material changes. Privacy notices carry the Article 13/14 information set in plain language (Article 12(1)); the privacy notice's processor and data-category lists are the same data as the registers, so a register change produces a document diff for review. Sector/feature annexes (payments, UGC, minors) attach only when the feature exists.
**Key rules.**
- A new processor, data category, or purpose without a corresponding document update blocks release — documents trail the registers, never lead or lag silently.
- Template residue is a defect class: no other company's name, no bracketed placeholders, no clauses for features the product does not have (the anatomy's slop line, made testable).
- Acceptance is an event, not a sentence: "by using this site you agree" without a stored version + timestamp record does not satisfy the skill.
- Documents are written for this product's actual data and features in language a non-lawyer user can parse; legal review by counsel is a recorded step for the initial version and material changes.
- Superseded versions remain retrievable: a dispute is answered with the exact text the user accepted.
**Gates.**
- *Automatable:* document-vs-register diff — processors and data categories named in the privacy notice are diffed against the processor register and RoPA in CI; any mismatch fails.
- *Automatable:* acceptance-record test — creating a test account asserts an acceptance row exists with document version and timestamp; template-residue lint scans published documents for placeholder patterns and foreign brand names.
**Sources.** GDPR Articles 12–14 (EUR-Lex text) — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng/ ; PROJECT_ANATOMY.md Part 6, Legal and compliance element (verify step: read live terms against the actual data inventory; confirm acceptance recorded with version and timestamp).

### 3.5 cookie-consent

**Core method.** Inventory every cookie, SDK, and tag and classify it: strictly necessary (exempt) vs. everything else (consent-gated). Non-essential storage/access does not fire before consent — enforcement is in the tag-loading layer, not in banner copy. The banner offers accept, reject, and granular purposes at equal prominence on the first layer; no pre-ticked toggles (*Planet49*), no implied consent by scrolling or continued browsing (ICO), no reject path buried deeper than the accept path. The consent event is recorded (subject/pseudonymous ID, timestamp, banner version, per-purpose choices) and honored by every downstream tag; withdrawal is reachable in the same number of steps as giving consent and takes effect on the next page load. New purposes or new third parties trigger fresh consent. For CCPA-scope products, the parallel mechanism is the opt-out of sale/sharing plus honoring opt-out preference signals, recorded the same way.
**Key rules.**
- Default state is off: an unclassified tracker is treated as non-essential and blocked until classified.
- Banner design is a compliance surface: visual emphasis that steers toward accept (size, color, repetition) is a defect, reviewed like copy.
- Consent state is a runtime input to analytics and marketing code, tested like any other feature flag — a tag that fires in the rejected state is a release blocker.
- The cookie inventory, the banner's purpose list, and the privacy notice's cookie section are one dataset rendered three ways; drift between them fails review.
- Consent records are retained and queryable per subject — they are also DSAR evidence.
**Gates.**
- *Automatable:* pre-consent network scan — a headless browser loads key pages with no interaction; the gate fails if any non-essential cookie is set or any non-essential third-party request fires.
- *Automatable:* consent-state test matrix — scripted accept-all / reject-all / partial / withdraw runs assert exactly which tags fire in each state, including that withdrawal stops previously allowed tags.
**Sources.** ICO, "Cookies and similar technologies" — https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guide-to-pecr/cookies-and-similar-technologies/ ; *Planet49*, C-673/17 (CJEU) — pre-ticked consent invalid, GDPR standard applies to cookies.

### 3.6 licensing

**Core method.** Build the component inventory from the SBOM plus a source-level scan (metadata misses vendored files, fonts, images, and pasted snippets): every component gets an SPDX license identifier and a tier decision from the project's written license policy (allowed / review / forbidden, defined per distribution model — hosted service vs. distributed artifact). Review-tier items get a named human decision recorded with reasoning; forbidden-tier items are replaced or carry a written exception. Attribution is discharged by a generated notices file shipped with the product, and copyleft source-offer duties, where triggered, are implemented as a real download/disclosure, not a link to upstream. The project's own LICENSE is chosen deliberately at creation (Choose a License) and names this project. Dependency additions re-run the pipeline — licensing is a CI property, not a launch-week audit.
**Key rules.**
- "No license found" is forbidden-tier: default copyright applies; absence of a license is not permission.
- Vendored code keeps its license and copyright files; stripping them is a defect (the anatomy's slop line).
- Fonts, stock assets, and copied snippets are components too — they enter the inventory with their terms (transferability checked, e.g. stock licenses that don't transfer to a client or a rebuild).
- The tier of a copyleft license is decided against the actual distribution model; the same library can be allowed in a hosted service and forbidden in a shipped binary.
- Exceptions expire: a written exception names the component, the reason, and a review date.
**Gates.**
- *Automatable:* CI license scan (ScanCode Toolkit for source-level evidence, or the ecosystem checker — `license-checker`, `pip-licenses`, `cargo-deny`) against the policy allowlist; a forbidden or unlisted SPDX identifier fails the build.
- *Automatable:* SBOM emitted per release (SPDX or CycloneDX) and diffed against the notices file — every shipped component appears in the notices.
**Sources.** SPDX License List — https://spdx.org/licenses/ ; Choose a License — https://choosealicense.com/ ; OpenChain ISO/IEC 5230 (license-compliance program standard).

### 3.7 compliance-audit

**Core method.** Run a scheduled, evidence-based audit against the obligations register: for each obligation, name the control, the test, the evidence artifact, and the last test date; sample real flows rather than reading policies (submit the DSAR drill, run the consent matrix, run the license scan, pull an acceptance record, pull an audit-trail entry). Findings get severity, owner, and remediation date, and are tracked to closure like bugs. The audit also sweeps public surfaces — marketing pages, app-store listings, sales decks — for compliance claims, and every claim must map to a tested control or be removed. Voluntary frameworks (SOC 2, ISO/IEC 27001) are audited as what they are — customer-trust certifications with their own scopes — and never reported as legal compliance. Audit scope and results are themselves records (audit-trails).
**Key rules.**
- Evidence beats assertion: a control with no dated test artifact is recorded as failing, whatever the policy says.
- The audit tests flows, not documents: documents are inputs; the pass/fail evidence comes from executing the real user paths.
- Public claims are in scope: "GDPR compliant", "PCI certified", "COPPA safe" and equivalents are findings unless an artifact supports the exact wording.
- Findings are not closed by intention: closure requires re-running the failed test and filing the new evidence.
- Cadence is risk-based and recorded: at minimum on a fixed schedule and on any trigger event (new market, new processor class, new regulated feature, legal change flagged by privacy-laws).
**Gates.**
- *Automatable:* evidence-freshness report — every control in the register lists its last-test date; any control past its freshness window appears as an open finding automatically.
- *Automatable:* claims scan — published marketing/legal pages are scanned against the approved-claims list; unapproved compliance terminology fails the check.
**Sources.** ICO data protection audit framework (accountability toolkit) — https://cy.ico.org.uk/for-organisations/advice-and-services/audits/data-protection-audit-framework/toolkits/accountability/records-of-processing-and-lawful-basis/ ; GDPR Articles 5(2) and 24 (demonstrability) — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng/

### 3.8 age-requirements

**Core method.** The age regime is per-market configuration: a table maps each market to its consent age and regime (GDPR Article 8: 16 default, member states 13–16 — e.g. Germany 16, France 15, Spain 14, Belgium/Sweden 13; UK 13; US COPPA: under 13 with verifiable parental consent). Where a product is child-directed or has actual knowledge of child users, collection is gated *before* personal data is taken: a neutral age screen (no coaching, no design that invites falsification), under-threshold users routed to a parental-consent flow using a regime-accepted method (COPPA: signed form, notifying payment transaction, trained-staff call/video, ID check with prompt deletion, knowledge-based authentication; GDPR: "reasonable efforts" to verify parental authorisation), or blocked where no consent path exists. Age data is minimised: store the threshold determination and its basis, not a full date of birth, unless a purpose requires it. Parents get review, deletion, and consent-withdrawal paths (COPPA), wired into dsar-handling. Teen-specific state laws and design codes (UK Children's Code) are register entries, flagged as variance, not silently ignored.
**Key rules.**
- The threshold is never a hardcoded constant; adding a market means adding its table row, verified against the national rule at that date.
- The age screen is neutral: pre-filled adult dates, "are you over X?" single-tap-throughs on a child-directed service, and retry-until-pass designs are defects.
- Parental consent is verifiable and recorded like any consent (audit-trails); a checkbox "I am the parent" alone is not a verification method.
- Under-threshold data discovered after the fact (actual knowledge) triggers the same flow retroactively: parental consent or deletion — it is never grandfathered.
- Age-gate data is itself personal data: minimised, retention-limited, and never reused for marketing or profiling.
**Gates.**
- *Automatable:* age-flow test suite — scripted signups per market config: under-threshold test users are blocked or routed to the parental flow, at-threshold users proceed; the suite runs against every market row in the table.
- *Automatable:* consent-record assertion — completing the parental flow in staging asserts a stored consent record with method, timestamp, and scope; completing signup as an under-threshold user without one is impossible by construction and tested as such.
**Sources.** GDPR Article 8 (EUR-Lex text) — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng/ ; FTC, Children's Privacy / COPPA guidance (incl. amended Rule, compliance from 22 April 2026) — https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/privacy-security/childrens-privacy

### 3.9 audit-trails

**Core method.** Every regulated action writes an append-only trail entry at the service layer: consent grants and withdrawals, policy acceptances, DSAR lifecycle events, data exports, refunds and billing adjustments, admin access to personal data, permission changes, and D14 rights/authorization decisions (including clone-ethics records). Entries carry actor, action, subject, trusted timestamp, and the version/reference of what was decided (document version, consent scope, rights decision). The store is tamper-evident — append-only storage, hash-chaining or WORM where the risk warrants — and access-controlled, because the trail itself contains personal data and gets its own retention schedule per obligation (e.g. CCPA request records ≥ 24 months; consent records for as long as the processing they justify continues, plus the limitation period the register specifies). Trails are reviewed on a cadence (compliance-audit samples them), and "the action happened but no entry exists" is treated as the action not having validly happened.
**Key rules.**
- No regulated action ships without its trail entry: the entry is written in the same transaction/flow as the action, not reconstructed later.
- Entries are never edited or deleted in place; corrections are new entries referencing the original.
- Timestamps come from a trusted server-side clock; client-supplied times are recorded as claims, not facts.
- The trail records the *basis* of decisions (which rule, which register row, which approver), not just the outcome — an outcome without a basis fails audit.
- Trail access is itself trailed: reading the audit log is a logged event.
**Gates.**
- *Automatable:* action-coverage test — in staging, perform each regulated action (accept policy, grant/withdraw consent, file and close a DSAR, issue a refund, export data, record a rights decision) and assert a complete trail entry exists for each; any missing entry fails.
- *Automatable:* tamper-evidence check — a scheduled job verifies the hash chain / append-only integrity of the trail store and alerts on any gap or mismatch.
**Sources.** GDPR Articles 5(2), 7(1), 30 (demonstrability and records) — https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng/ ; CPPA CCPA Regulations §7101 (request records) — https://cppa.ca.gov/regulations/pdf/20230329_final_regs_text.pdf

### 3.10 clone-ethics

**Core method.** D14 owns the decisions; D16 executes them. No clone work — not even discovery crawling — starts before a written **authorization record** exists in the project file: who authorized, their right to authorize (own site, licensed target), scope, and date; the record must predate the first crawl log entry (D16 site-discovery handoff). Rights decisions are made per asset class in the harvesting manifest — `owned`, `licensed`, `recreate`, `replace` — prepared with evidence by D16 and **approved by D14**; `licensed` in particular is a legal judgment (license scope, transferability, territory) that the harvesting skill can never self-approve (D16 open risk, adopted here as a rule). Layout patterns and behavior may be recreated; trademarks, logos, and copyrighted text and images are never copied verbatim, and competitor clones ship with different branding and content. Crawl conduct: identified crawler, robots.txt honored in full, rate limits respected, no login or paywall bypass, no borrowed credentials — and robots.txt silence is never read as permission (RFC 9309: robots rules "are not a form of access authorization"). Targets whose robots rules exclude archiving route to D14 for an explicit decision rather than a workaround (D16 handoff). Pages D16 marks designed-not-cloned carry no parity claim, in the delivery report or anywhere else.
**Key rules.**
- Authorization precedes access: the dated record exists before the first request to the target; retroactive authorization is recorded as a finding, not a cure.
- Every asset ships with a D14-approved rights decision; an undecided asset defaults to `replace` (D16's rule, enforced by this department).
- Verbatim copying of trademarks, logos, or copyrighted text/images is forbidden regardless of authorization scope, unless the authorization explicitly covers that exact reuse (e.g. the client's own brand assets in their own migration).
- The authorization record and rights decisions are audit-trail entries (audit-trails): approver, date, basis.
- If authorization is ambiguous — agency cloning a client's site, a former vendor's build, a franchise — work stops at inventory (URLs only, no harvesting) until the rights position is resolved in writing.
**Gates.**
- *Automatable:* authorization-vs-crawl check — the project's authorization record timestamp is compared against the first crawl log timestamp; crawl-first fails the gate outright.
- *Automatable:* brand-residue grep — the rebuild is scanned for source brand names, trademarked terms, and harvested-asset hashes outside the approved-references list; hits block delivery until each is cleared or replaced. Manifest completeness: every asset file has a row with a D14-approved decision (shared gate with D16 asset-harvesting).
**Sources.** RFC 9309, Robots Exclusion Protocol — https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9309.html ; PROJECT_ANATOMY.md Part 10, Clone ethics element, and docs/internal/research/D16.md ([→ D14] handoffs).

## 4. Open risks for Phase 3

- **Clone-ethics ownership vs. single-agent execution.** D16 hands authorization and rights calls to D14, and §3.10 makes D14 the approver — but OmniForge runs as a single agent, so "another department approves" can degenerate into the same run rubber-stamping its own evidence. Phase 3 must build a forcing function: the clone-ethics skill should require the authorization record and rights decisions to exist as *pre-committed artifacts* (dated files the crawl tooling checks before running), so the gate is structural, not a self-attestation written after the fact. The `licensed` decision especially must be impossible for the harvesting flow to self-approve.
- **Legal drift is guaranteed.** The CCPA regulations were materially updated effective 1 January 2026 with further rulemaking (automated decision-making technology) in progress; the amended COPPA Rule's compliance date landed 22 April 2026; EU adequacy decisions are renewed and expire (the UK's was renewed in December 2025); member-state Article 8 ages change by national statute. Skills that hardcode a deadline, an age, or an adequacy list as prose will silently rot. Phase 3 should put every such value in a dated, sourced table with a review trigger, and compliance-audit must treat stale tables as findings.
- **Jurisdiction variance can explode scope.** A full per-market treatment of privacy law is unbounded (US state patchwork, sector laws, non-EU regimes). Phase 3 needs an explicit scope rule: ship deep profiles for GDPR and CCPA, a register format plus worked examples for the rest, and a hard "register before serving" gate for unprofiled markets — otherwise drafting either balloons or, worse, implies coverage it doesn't have.
- **The "not legal advice" boundary under autonomous execution.** The framing rule (§ header) is easy to print and hard to honor: the skills must encode *escalation criteria* — children's products, health/financial data, biometric processing, contested clone rights, regulator contact — where the correct output is "stop, this needs counsel," and Phase 3 should test that the skills actually stop there rather than producing confident guidance.
- **Legal-documents depends on other departments' artifacts.** Documents are generated from the RoPA, processor register, and fact-sheet; if D1/D5/D10 artifacts are thin or stale, legal-documents degrades into exactly the generic-template slop it exists to prevent. Phase 3 should make the document skill *fail loudly* on missing register inputs instead of drafting around them.
- **Audit trails are themselves personal data.** The trail concentrates sensitive metadata (who consented, who was erased, who accessed what). Retention, access control, and DSAR treatment of the trail itself are only sketched in §3.9; Phase 3 must resolve the recursion (a DSAR can reach the trail; erasure can conflict with trail retention duties) per regime rather than globally.
- **Consent enforcement lives outside D14.** Cookie-consent's gates assume D12/D9 instrumentation honors consent state and D3 implements the banner accessibly and symmetrically. If those departments treat consent as a UI widget instead of a runtime signal, D14's controls are decorative; Phase 3 drafting should cross-reference the consent-state contract explicitly in all three departments' skills.
