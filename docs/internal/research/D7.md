# D7 Research — Security (Phase 2)

Purpose: the research base for drafting the 14 D7 skills in Phase 3. Every claim below traces to PROJECT_ANATOMY.md (Part 6 "Security" and "Secrets management"; Part 11 "Authentication and authorization implementation", "Input validation", "Rate limiting and abuse controls", "Admin and internal tools", "Content moderation and trust & safety"), a DET parameter file, or a source verified by web search on 2026-11-21. Audience: Phase 3 skill drafters for D7.

## 1. Department standard

Professional application-security practice today, as D7 will teach it:

- **Two OWASP references, two different jobs.** The OWASP Top 10:2025 is the awareness checklist: A01 Broken Access Control (now including SSRF), A02 Security Misconfiguration, A03 Software Supply Chain Failures, A04 Cryptographic Failures, A05 Injection, A06 Insecure Design, A07 Authentication Failures, A08 Software or Data Integrity Failures, A09 Security Logging and Alerting Failures, A10 Mishandling of Exceptional Conditions. The OWASP ASVS 5.0.0 (released 30 May 2025; 17 chapters, ~345 requirements, three cumulative levels L1/L2/L3) is the verification standard: requirements are cited with versioned IDs (e.g. `v5.0.0-1.2.5`) and traced to tests. Top 10 tells you what to fear; ASVS tells you what to verify. Sources: OWASP Top 10, https://owasp.org/www-project-top-ten/ ; OWASP ASVS, https://asvs.owasp.org/
- **Passwords follow NIST SP 800-63B Rev. 4 (final, July 2025), not folklore.** Length over complexity: at least 15 characters when the password is the only factor, a floor of 8 when it is one factor of MFA; verifiers must support at least 64 characters, must not impose composition rules, must not force periodic changes (change on evidence of compromise only), must check new passwords against a blocklist of breached, common, dictionary, and context-specific values, must allow password managers and paste, and must rate-limit failed attempts. Storage is a salted, cost-factored hash — never reversible, never a fast general-purpose hash alone. Source: NIST SP 800-63B Rev. 4, https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63b.html (guidance summary verified via web search 2026-11-21).
- **APIs are secured against their own list.** The OWASP API Security Top 10 (2023) is authorization-dominated: API1 Broken Object Level Authorization, API2 Broken Authentication, API3 Broken Object Property Level Authorization, API4 Unrestricted Resource Consumption, API5 Broken Function Level Authorization, API6 Unrestricted Access to Sensitive Business Flows, API7 SSRF, API8 Security Misconfiguration, API9 Improper Inventory Management, API10 Unsafe Consumption of APIs. Three of the ten are authorization failures that scanners cannot judge — they need per-role, per-object tests. Source: OWASP API Security Top 10 (2023), summary at https://orca.security/resources/blog/owasp-api-security-top-10/
- **Supply chain is a build property, not a scan afterthought.** Dependencies are pinned by lockfile, inventoried in an SBOM (SPDX or CycloneDX), scanned against vulnerability data (OSV), and updated through reviewed automation. Build integrity follows SLSA (spec v1.2, November 2025): Build L1 provenance exists, L2 a hosted build platform signs the provenance, L3 a hardened, isolated build platform; a Source Track was added in v1.2. SLSA proves where an artifact came from, not that its code is safe — both halves are needed. Sources: SLSA levels, https://slsa.dev/spec/v1.0/levels ; SLSA v1.2 status, https://socket.dev/glossary/supply-chain-levels-for-software-artifacts
- **PCI DSS scope is an architecture decision, and "compliance" is a contract, not a law.** PCI DSS v4.0.1 is the only active version (its future-dated requirements became mandatory 31 March 2025). The standard is written by the PCI Security Standards Council but enforced through merchant agreements by the card brands and the acquirer, who has the final word on validation. Scope follows the card data: a fully outsourced hosted payment page or provider-served iframe keeps a merchant at SAQ A (the shortest questionnaire); a merchant-built form that posts to the gateway is SAQ A-EP; card data touching your own systems is generally SAQ D. Since the January 2025 SAQ A revision, even SAQ A merchants must confirm their site is protected against script attacks that could affect the payment page. Never print, log, or render a full card number anywhere, including admin tools. Sources: PCI DSS v4.0.1 / SAQ structure, https://flopay.co/guides/pci-compliance-for-small-business.html ; SAQ A 2025 revision detail, https://scandiweb.com/blog/pci-dss-saq-2025-updates-for-ecommerce-merchants/ ; PROJECT_ANATOMY.md, "Admin and internal tools" (PCI scope note).
- **Threat modeling happens before code, per feature.** A written model per major feature: assets and trust boundaries on a data-flow diagram, threats enumerated with a method (STRIDE or equivalent), abuse cases beside use cases, and each accepted threat traced to a control, a test, or a documented risk acceptance. OWASP's Threat Modeling Cheat Sheet and Threat Dragon are the department's tooling baseline. Source: OWASP Cheat Sheet Series, "Threat Modeling Cheat Sheet", https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Threat_Modeling_Cheat_Sheet.html
- **Testing is tiered and authorized.** SAST and dependency scanning run in CI on every change; DAST (OWASP ZAP baseline) runs against staging; penetration testing follows the OWASP Web Security Testing Guide under written authorization, on systems the project owner owns or is contractually permitted to test, with defined scope, window, and data-handling rules. An audit verifies controls against ASVS; a penetration test tries to break them — the skills must never blur the two. Source: OWASP ZAP and anatomy "Security" verify step; OWASP Web Security Testing Guide, https://owasp.org/www-project-web-security-testing-guide/

## 3. Per-skill research notes

### owasp
- **Core method:** Run the OWASP Top 10:2025 as a release checklist, not a poster: for each category, name the control this project implements, where it lives in code/config, and the test that proves it. The standing controls: parameterized queries and contextual output encoding (A05), server-side authorization on every object and function (A01), hardened configuration with debug surfaces off in production (A02), vetted dependencies (A03, with supply-chain), AEAD encryption and hashed passwords (A04), threat modeling for design flaws (A06), session and login hardening (A07, with auth-hardening), signed/verified artifacts (A08, with supply-chain), security event logging with alerting (A09), and fail-closed error paths (A10). ASVS 5.0.0 L1 is the minimum bar for a public app; L2 for anything holding payments or sensitive personal data.
- **Key rules:**
  1. Every Top 10 category gets a named control and a named test before release; "not applicable" requires a written reason, not silence.
  2. Injection defense is parameterization plus allowlist validation — never escaping by hand or denylist regexes (DET-CODE-017, DET-CODE-019).
  3. Errors fail closed: a thrown exception in an authorization, payment, or moderation path denies the action, never permits it (DET-CODE-005).
  4. No security control may be disabled by configuration in production; a disabled control is a release blocker (DET-CODE-020).
  5. Verbose errors, stack traces, and framework banners never reach the client (A02/A10).
- **Verification gates:** Top 10 checklist diff review per release with ASVS requirement IDs cited (manual, checklist-driven); OWASP ZAP baseline scan in CI with zero high findings (automatable); SAST rule pack (injection, unsafe deserialization, disabled controls) gating merges (automatable).
- **Sources:** OWASP Top 10:2025, https://owasp.org/www-project-top-ten/ ; OWASP ASVS 5.0.0, https://asvs.owasp.org/

### session-security
- **Core method:** Sessions are server-issued, high-entropy identifiers (at least 128 bits of entropy from a CSPRNG) stored server-side or in a signed, server-verifiable token. A new session ID is issued at authentication and at every privilege change; the pre-login ID is discarded. Cookies carry `Secure`, `HttpOnly`, and `SameSite=Lax` (or `Strict` where flows allow). Both an idle timeout and an absolute timeout apply; logout invalidates the session server-side, not just in the browser. Concurrent-session and device visibility for users holding sensitive accounts.
- **Key rules:**
  1. Never accept a session ID the client invented, and never put session IDs in URLs.
  2. Rotation on login and privilege escalation is mandatory — session fixation is a designed-out bug, not a finding (anatomy "Auth implementation", OWASP Session Management Cheat Sheet).
  3. Cookie attributes are set explicitly per cookie; framework defaults are verified, not assumed.
  4. Logout and password change invalidate all other sessions for that account, or the user is told plainly that they do not.
  5. Remember-me is a separate, revocable, long-lived token with its own storage — never a stretched session.
- **Verification gates:** Session test pack — fixation attempt, replay after logout, expired-ID reuse, cookie-attribute assertions on every Set-Cookie (automatable); session configuration lint against the project session policy in CI (automatable).
- **Sources:** OWASP Cheat Sheet Series, "Session Management Cheat Sheet", https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Session_Management_Cheat_Sheet.html ; OWASP ASVS 5.0.0 ch. V7 Session Management, https://asvs.owasp.org/

### api-security
- **Core method:** Secure the API against the OWASP API Security Top 10 (2023) directly: every request is authenticated, every object access is authorized against the caller's ownership (BOLA/API1), every function checks the caller's role (BFLA/API5), responses and writes are filtered at property level (BOPLA/API3 — no mass assignment, no over-exposure), resource consumption is capped per endpoint (API4), sensitive business flows have anti-automation controls (API6), outbound fetches of user-supplied URLs are allowlisted and network-restricted (SSRF/API7), the full endpoint inventory — including old versions — is known and owned (API9), and data consumed from third-party APIs is validated like any other input (API10). Keys and tokens are scoped, rotatable, and never shipped in client bundles (DET-INF-006).
- **Key rules:**
  1. An object ID in a request is a claim, not a permission: the server looks up ownership from the session, never from a client-supplied owner field.
  2. Authorization tests exist per role × per endpoint, including cross-tenant ID swaps; a scanner cannot substitute for them.
  3. Response serializers are explicit allowlists of fields — returning the ORM object is a BOPLA finding.
  4. Every deployed API version is in the inventory with an owner and a retirement date; undocumented endpoints are treated as incidents (API9).
  5. Server-side URL fetching denies private/loopback/link-local ranges and re-validates after redirects (API7).
- **Verification gates:** Authorization matrix test suite — each role requests each protected endpoint with own, foreign, and missing credentials, asserting 401/403 and no data leakage (automatable); API inventory diff — live route listing vs. documented inventory — in CI (automatable); ZAP API scan against the OpenAPI definition (automatable).
- **Sources:** OWASP API Security Top 10 (2023), https://orca.security/resources/blog/owasp-api-security-top-10/ ; PROJECT_ANATOMY.md, "Authentication and authorization implementation"

### auth-hardening
- **Core method:** Build login, registration, recovery, and MFA to NIST SP 800-63B Rev. 4 and OWASP's Authentication Cheat Sheet: password policy is length + blocklist (no composition rules, no scheduled rotation); credential stuffing and brute force are met with per-account and per-IP rate limiting, progressive delays, and breached-password screening at set/reset; MFA (TOTP or WebAuthn/passkeys preferred over SMS) is offered and pushed for privileged accounts; recovery flows are as strong as login — single-use, short-lived, unguessable tokens, notified to the account holder, and never revealing whether an account exists. All auth events (success, failure, reset, MFA change) are logged with alerting on anomalies (A09:2025).
- **Key rules:**
  1. Password minimums: 15 characters single-factor, 8 within MFA; accept at least 64; allow paste and password managers (NIST 800-63B-4).
  2. No knowledge-based recovery ("security questions") and no hints readable by unauthenticated claimants (NIST 800-63B-4).
  3. Login, reset, and MFA endpoints have the strictest rate limits in the product, and limits key on account + IP, not a spoofable header alone (DET-INF-023).
  4. Error messages never distinguish "no such account" from "wrong password"; timing differences are flattened where feasible.
  5. Privilege changes (role grant, email change, MFA reset) require re-authentication and notify the account holder out-of-band.
- **Verification gates:** Auth abuse test pack — credential-stuffing burst, reset-token replay and expiry, enumeration probes, blocklist rejection of known-breached passwords (automatable); NIST policy conformance checklist diffed against the live auth configuration (automatable config assertion).
- **Sources:** NIST SP 800-63B Rev. 4, https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63b.html ; OWASP Cheat Sheet Series, "Authentication Cheat Sheet", https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Authentication_Cheat_Sheet.html

### secrets-management
- **Core method:** Secrets live outside code from the first commit: environment variables or a dedicated secret manager, injected at deploy time, scoped per environment and per service, least-privilege, and never logged. The repo ships `.env.example` with names and placeholder values only. Gitleaks (or equivalent) runs as a pre-commit hook and a CI gate over the full git history, not just the working tree. Rotation is scheduled and immediate on suspicion; exposure response is revoke-first, investigate-second, because a committed secret must be treated as public — deleting the line does not remove it from history (anatomy "Secrets management").
- **Key rules:**
  1. Zero real values in the repository, its history, CI logs, client bundles, or chat — placeholders are obviously fake and paired with reserved example hosts where a URL is needed (DET-CODE-018, DET-INF-005, DET-INF-006).
  2. One credential per service per environment; a master key reused across environments is a finding, not a shortcut.
  3. `.env` is git-ignored from commit one; `git check-ignore .env` must succeed in every project template.
  4. Client-side code contains no secret by definition — anything shipped to a browser is public; privileged calls go through the server.
  5. Every secret has an owner, a rotation date, and a revocation path that has been tested, not just documented.
- **Verification gates:** `gitleaks git .` over full history in CI, zero findings required (automatable); bundle scan asserting no secret patterns in shipped client assets (automatable); `.env.example` diff check — names only, no value that parses as a live credential (automatable).
- **Sources:** Gitleaks, https://github.com/gitleaks/gitleaks ; PROJECT_ANATOMY.md, "Secrets management"

### dependency-safety
- **Core method:** Dependencies are a managed inventory, not an install-time accident: a committed lockfile pins every direct and transitive version; an SBOM (CycloneDX or SPDX) is generated in CI per release; vulnerability scanning (OSV-Scanner, `npm audit`, `pip-audit`, or the stack equivalent) runs in CI and on a schedule against the shipped manifest; update automation (Dependabot/Renovate) opens reviewable PRs, with security fixes fast-tracked and everything else batched. New dependencies pass an adoption check: maintained, license-compatible (with D14 licensing), and actually needed. Base images are pinned and rebuilt on a cadence (DET-INF-025).
- **Key rules:**
  1. The lockfile is the build input; a build that resolves versions at install time is unreviewed by definition (DET-CODE-028).
  2. A critical or high vulnerability in a reachable dependency blocks release; unreachable or disputed findings need a written, dated analysis (e.g. a VEX note), not a muted alert.
  3. Install scripts and postinstall hooks of new dependencies are treated as code execution and reviewed as such.
  4. Abandoned or typosquat-adjacent packages (name lookalikes of popular packages) are rejected at adoption review.
  5. Scanners run against what ships — production manifest and final image — not only the dev tree.
- **Verification gates:** CI vulnerability scan with severity threshold gate (automatable); lockfile-presence and lockfile-diff checks on every PR (automatable); scheduled rescan of the last released artifact with alerting (automatable).
- **Sources:** OSV, https://osv.dev/ ; OWASP Top 10:2025 A03 Software Supply Chain Failures, https://owasp.org/www-project-top-ten/

### supply-chain
- **Core method:** Extend dependency-safety from "what we use" to "how what we ship was built": builds run on a hosted, access-controlled CI platform from version-controlled source — never from a developer laptop for release. Following SLSA (v1.2): produce provenance for every release artifact (Build L1), have the build platform sign it (L2), and prefer hardened, isolated build runners (L3) for high-value targets. Release artifacts are immutable and pinned by digest (DET-INF-026); consumers and deploy steps verify signatures/digests before running anything. Source-side: protected branches, required review, and signed commits or tags where the platform supports them; the Source Track (new in SLSA v1.2) grades the repository side.
- **Key rules:**
  1. If it cannot be rebuilt from a named commit by the build platform, it does not ship.
  2. Provenance is generated by the build platform, not written by the release engineer — self-attested provenance is documentation, not evidence.
  3. Third-party CI actions and build plugins are pinned by full commit SHA or digest, not by mutable tags.
  4. A compromised-credential drill exists: revoke, rotate, and re-issue artifacts — in that order — and it has been rehearsed.
  5. SLSA claims are stated precisely ("Build L2") and only for artifacts the pipeline actually produced that way; provenance does not claim the code is safe, only where it came from.
- **Verification gates:** Provenance presence + signature verification step in the release pipeline that fails the release on absence (automatable); artifact digest comparison between build output and deployed artifact (automatable); CI configuration lint for unpinned actions and laptop-deploy paths (automatable).
- **Sources:** SLSA specification and levels, https://slsa.dev/spec/v1.0/levels ; SLSA v1.2 (Source Track added, November 2025), https://socket.dev/glossary/supply-chain-levels-for-software-artifacts

### encryption
- **Core method:** Encrypt in transit and at rest with vetted, standard constructions only — no hand-rolled cryptography. Transit: TLS 1.2 minimum, TLS 1.3 preferred, configured from a maintained profile (Mozilla SSL Configuration Generator), HSTS after HTTPS is proven, HTTP redirected, certificates issued and renewed automatically (ACME) with expiry alerting (DET-INF-014/015/016). At rest: AEAD ciphers (AES-256-GCM or ChaCha20-Poly1305) with keys from a KMS or platform key store, envelope encryption for per-record needs, and keys separated from the data they protect. Passwords are not encrypted — they are hashed with a dedicated password KDF (see auth-hardening; OWASP Password Storage Cheat Sheet: Argon2id first choice, then scrypt/bcrypt/PBKDF2 with current parameters). Tokens and reset links are stored hashed, like passwords.
- **Key rules:**
  1. Never design a cipher, protocol, or key-exchange; if the library does not offer it as a standard option, the design is wrong.
  2. ECB mode, static IVs, MD5/SHA1 for security purposes, and home-grown obfuscation are findings on sight (A04:2025).
  3. Keys never live in source, config files in the repo, or the same store as the ciphertext; key access is logged.
  4. Certificate expiry is monitored with alerts well before the date — an expired cert is an operations failure, not bad luck (DET-INF-016).
  5. Downgrade paths are closed: no plaintext fallback for sensitive traffic, no mixed content on secure pages.
- **Verification gates:** TLS scan of the deployed endpoint against the project profile (testssl.sh or SSL Labs) in the release checklist (automatable); certificate-expiry monitor with alert threshold (automatable); codebase scan for banned primitives and modes (automatable SAST rules).
- **Sources:** OWASP Cheat Sheet Series, "Cryptographic Storage Cheat Sheet" and "Transport Layer Security Cheat Sheet", https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Cryptographic_Storage_Cheat_Sheet.html ; Mozilla SSL Configuration Generator, https://ssl-config.mozilla.org/

### security-headers
- **Core method:** Set a deliberate HTTP response header policy on every response, from a written per-project header sheet: Content-Security-Policy built from the app's real origins (starting report-only, then enforced; no `unsafe-inline` for scripts on authenticated apps), Strict-Transport-Security with a long max-age once HTTPS is universal, X-Content-Type-Options `nosniff`, Referrer-Policy, Permissions-Policy disabling unused device APIs, and clickjacking defense via CSP `frame-ancestors` (X-Frame-Options as legacy fallback). Headers are set once, centrally (edge or middleware), verified against the deployed site — not against local dev, where framework defaults differ (DET-INF-022).
- **Key rules:**
  1. CSP is an allowlist of real origins; a policy copied from a tutorial that permits CDNs the app does not use is a finding.
  2. HSTS is enabled only after every subdomain serves HTTPS; `includeSubDomains` and preload are deliberate, recorded decisions.
  3. Headers are verified on the production host with `curl -sI` (anatomy "Security" verify step) — staging and prod must match the sheet.
  4. Removing or weakening a header requires the same review as removing any other control.
  5. Error responses and API responses carry the same header policy as pages; headers set only by the HTML layer are incomplete.
- **Verification gates:** Header assertion test against the deployed URL in CI/release checks — expected values diffed against the header sheet (automatable); CSP report endpoint monitored for violations after each deploy (automatable alerting).
- **Sources:** OWASP Secure Headers Project, https://owasp.org/www-project-secure-headers/ ; PROJECT_ANATOMY.md, "Security"

### threat-modelling
- **Core method:** Model before building, per major feature: draw the data-flow diagram with trust boundaries, list the assets (credentials, personal data, money movement, admin capability), enumerate threats with STRIDE per element, write abuse cases next to the feature's use cases ("user views another tenant's invoice by changing an ID"), and decide per threat: control, test, or written risk acceptance with an owner and a review date. The model is a living project file, updated when the feature changes, and its outputs feed concrete work: ASVS requirements selected, authorization tests written, DET parameters to re-check. A06:2025 Insecure Design is the category this skill exists to prevent — flaws no scanner finds because the code does exactly what was designed.
- **Key rules:**
  1. No threat model, no build, for features that touch auth, payments, personal data, file upload, or admin capability.
  2. Every trust-boundary crossing on the diagram gets at least one named threat and one named control.
  3. Risk acceptances expire; an undated, unowned acceptance is treated as an open threat.
  4. Abuse cases are written in the attacker's verbs (steal, enumerate, replay, escalate), not the system's.
  5. The model names what is out of scope honestly (e.g. "hosting provider compromise not modeled") instead of implying total coverage.
- **Verification gates:** Threat-model presence and freshness check in the feature's definition of done — file exists, dated within the current work cycle, threats traced to controls/tests/acceptances (manual, checklist-driven); traceability spot-check — pick three threats, find their controls in code or their tests (manual).
- **Sources:** OWASP Cheat Sheet Series, "Threat Modeling Cheat Sheet", https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Threat_Modeling_Cheat_Sheet.html ; OWASP Threat Dragon, https://owasp.org/www-project-threat-dragon/

### security-audit
- **Core method:** A security audit is verification against a standard, performed on evidence: select the ASVS 5.0.0 level appropriate to the product (L1 public apps, L2 sensitive data/payments), work the applicable requirements one by one, and for each record pass / fail / not-applicable-with-reason plus the evidence (test output, config excerpt, code location). The audit combines automated evidence (SAST, dependency scan, ZAP, header and TLS checks) with manual review of the things tools cannot judge: authorization logic, business-flow abuse, session design. Findings are severity-rated by exploitability and impact, each with an owner and a fix-or-accept decision; the audit report states its scope and its limits in the first section. An audit is not a penetration test and must not be sold as one.
- **Key rules:**
  1. Every ASVS requirement in scope ends in a recorded verdict with evidence; sampling is declared where used, never silent.
  2. Findings cite the requirement ID (versioned, e.g. `v5.0.0-…`) and the DET parameter where one exists, so fixes are checkable.
  3. A failed requirement with no owner and no date is reported as an open risk in the summary, not buried in an appendix.
  4. The audit verifies the deployed configuration, not only the repository — debug surfaces, headers, TLS, and defaults are checked live (DET-INF-021, DET-INF-022).
  5. Re-audit after remediation covers the failed items plus regression checks on adjacent controls.
- **Verification gates:** Audit artifact completeness check — every in-scope requirement has verdict + evidence + (on fail) owner and date (automatable over the report structure); evidence freshness rule — scanner outputs attached to the report must be dated within the audit window (automatable).
- **Sources:** OWASP ASVS 5.0.0, https://asvs.owasp.org/ ; PROJECT_ANATOMY.md, "Security"

### penetration-testing
- **Core method:** Penetration testing is authorized, scoped, adversarial simulation against a running system, following the OWASP Web Security Testing Guide's test cases: reconnaissance and inventory, authentication and session attacks, authorization and BOLA probing, injection, business-logic abuse, and configuration review — executed with the same techniques an attacker would use, within written rules of engagement. Every engagement starts from authorization: a signed scope naming the target systems, permitted techniques, testing window, data-handling and stop conditions, and the emergency contact. Findings are reproduced, evidenced, severity-rated, and retested after fixes. Testing production requires explicit additional consent and rate care; staging that faithfully mirrors production is the default target.
- **Key rules:**
  1. **No authorization, no test.** Testing is performed only on systems the project owner owns or is contractually engaged to test; third-party services, shared hosting neighbors, and providers' infrastructure are out of scope unless the engagement letter says otherwise.
  2. Scope is a list, not a vibe: domains, IPs, accounts, and techniques not named are not tested.
  3. Real user data encountered during a test is evidence to minimize — record the minimum proof, never exfiltrate datasets, and report exposure through the agreed channel immediately.
  4. Denial-of-service, social engineering, and physical techniques are excluded unless explicitly written into the engagement.
  5. Every critical/high finding ships with a reproduction an engineer can follow and a retest result after remediation.
- **Verification gates:** Engagement-file completeness check — authorization, scope, window, and rules of engagement on file before any tool runs (manual gate, blocking); retest pass rate reported per engagement — findings without retest are listed as unverified fixes (manual, report-driven).
- **Sources:** OWASP Web Security Testing Guide, https://owasp.org/www-project-web-security-testing-guide/ ; OWASP API Security Top 10 (2023) for API test targeting, https://orca.security/resources/blog/owasp-api-security-top-10/

### pci-compliance
- **Core method:** Engineer the product so card data never touches it: card entry happens on the payment provider's hosted page or provider-served hosted fields/iframe; the product stores and transmits tokens and the provider's transaction IDs, never the PAN. That architecture is what holds the merchant to SAQ A under PCI DSS v4.0.1; letting a merchant-built form or JavaScript handle card data moves the product to SAQ A-EP, and storing, processing, or transmitting card data on your own systems is SAQ D territory — a decision made by the integration design, not by paperwork afterwards. The skill's work: choose and document the integration class, keep PAN out of every surface (pages, admin tools, logs, analytics, support tickets, error reports), complete the correct SAQ honestly with the acquirer's confirmation, and repeat on the acquirer's cadence and after any integration change. Per the anatomy: admin tools sit inside the payment trust boundary whenever staff can view orders or trigger refunds, so they must never display or store full card numbers.
- **Key rules:**
  1. The full card number and CVV are never stored, logged, rendered, or transmitted by the product — in any field, including "notes" and debug output; CVV is never stored by anyone after authorization, including the provider pattern the product controls.
  2. The SAQ type is determined by the actual integration and confirmed with the acquirer; the acquirer and card brands, not the PCI SSC and not this skill, have the final word on validation duties.
  3. PCI DSS is a contractual obligation via the merchant agreement, not a statute — the skill must say so, and must not promise that SAQ A status equals legal compliance in any jurisdiction.
  4. Even at SAQ A, the merchant site that embeds or redirects to the payment page must be protected against script attacks that could affect the payment page (January 2025 SAQ A eligibility revision); third-party scripts on checkout pages are inventoried and minimized.
  5. Compliance claims in marketing copy ("PCI certified") are prohibited unless they match the filed attestation exactly (anatomy "Legal and compliance" slop); the provider's compliance does not transfer to the merchant.
- **Verification gates:** PAN-pattern scan (Luhn-checked card-number detection) over the codebase, database dumps, and log samples — zero hits required (automatable); checkout integration review — card fields provably served by the provider (DOM/source inspection) on every release that touches checkout (automatable + manual); SAQ currency check — attestation on file, dated within the acquirer's required cycle (manual gate).
- **Sources:** PCI DSS v4.0.1 and SAQ structure, https://flopay.co/guides/pci-compliance-for-small-business.html ; SAQ A January 2025 revision, https://scandiweb.com/blog/pci-dss-saq-2025-updates-for-ecommerce-merchants/ ; Stripe, "What is PCI DSS compliance?", https://stripe.com/guides/pci-compliance ; PROJECT_ANATOMY.md, "Admin and internal tools"

### content-moderation
- **Core method:** Run moderation as a system with four parts, per the Trust & Safety Professional Association curriculum: (1) policy in two forms — a public version users can read and an internal enforcement guide with examples per rule; (2) intake — in-product reporting on every piece of user content and every account, plus automated detection (spam, hash-matching, classifiers) feeding the same queue; (3) review — a queue with priority tiers (imminent harm and illegal content first), trained human review for context-dependent decisions, and a defined enforcement ladder: label, reduce visibility, remove, suspend, ban; (4) accountability — every action logged with the policy cited, an appeal path that reaches a reviewer, and periodic transparency reporting. Known-CSAM handling is a hard legal lane, not a policy choice: US-based providers must report apparent CSAM to the NCMEC CyberTipline (18 U.S.C. § 2258A), and platforms serving the EU carry notice-and-action and statement-of-reasons duties under the Digital Services Act — the skill must route these to D14 for jurisdiction confirmation rather than improvise.
- **Key rules:**
  1. A report button that does not reach a staffed queue with a response target is a defect, not a feature (anatomy slop line).
  2. No irreversible enforcement (ban, mass removal) by automated filter alone; automation queues and labels, humans decide the top of the ladder.
  3. Every enforcement action records actor, policy clause, evidence, and timestamp — unlogged actions are treated as not defensible (audit-trails, D14).
  4. Appeals are a real flow with a reviewer different from the original decision-maker where staffing allows, and outcomes feed policy-guide fixes.
  5. Moderator welfare and access control are part of the design: least-privilege queue access, blurring/warnings for graphic content, and no moderation tooling exposed outside the admin surface (DET-INF-021).
- **Verification gates:** End-to-end report drill — file a test report as a user, trace it through the queue to a decision and an appeal, asserting the log entries exist (automatable workflow test); queue SLA report — median time-to-first-review per priority tier against the policy targets (automatable); policy-coverage check — every report category in the UI maps to a clause in the public policy (manual, checklist-driven).
- **Sources:** Trust & Safety Professional Association, "Content Moderation Simulation" (T&S Curriculum), https://www.tspa.org/curriculum/ts-curriculum/content-moderation-simulation/ ; PROJECT_ANATOMY.md, "Content moderation and trust & safety"

## 4. Open risks for Phase 3

- **Audit vs. penetration test boundary.** The sharpest drafting risk in this department. A security-audit skill teaches verification against ASVS with evidence; a penetration-testing skill teaches adversarial technique. If Phase 3 blurs them, the repo will contain a "pentest" skill that is really a checklist, or an "audit" skill that encourages unauthenticated probing. Drafting rule: penetration-testing content must be unusable without its authorization gate — the engagement file (owner's written authorization, scope, window, rules of engagement) is step one of the method, not a footnote — and it must state plainly that only systems the project owner owns or is contractually engaged to test may be tested. Third-party infrastructure, other tenants, and payment providers are never implied targets.
- **Standards version drift.** This file pins OWASP Top 10:2025, ASVS 5.0.0, NIST SP 800-63B Rev. 4, PCI DSS v4.0.1, and SLSA v1.2 as of 2026-11-21. ASVS 5.0 renumbered nearly every requirement from 4.0.3, so any Phase 3 text that cites a requirement ID without its version prefix will rot silently. Drafting rule: every cited ID carries its version (`v5.0.0-…`, `A01:2025`, `API1:2023`), and skills teach the reader to check for a newer edition rather than treating the pinned one as eternal.
- **PCI scope honesty.** SAQ boundaries depend on the acquirer, the card brands, and the exact integration; hosted-field products differ in detail between providers, and the January 2025 SAQ A revision shows the questionnaire itself changes. The pci-compliance skill must teach scope determination and PAN exclusion as engineering, present SAQ A/A-EP/D as the typical mapping (not a guarantee), and hand the final determination to the acquirer — any draft that promises "use this pattern and you are compliant" is a defect.
- **Jurisdiction-dependent controls.** Moderation duties (NCMEC reporting in the US, DSA notice-and-action in the EU), breach-notification clocks, and data-handling rules vary by jurisdiction and by the product's markets. D7 skills should implement the technical controls (queues, logs, hash-matching hooks, export/delete support) and route the legal determination to D14 skills (privacy-laws, dsar-handling, compliance-audit) instead of stating legal conclusions.
- **Automation asymmetry.** Header, TLS, secret, and dependency gates automate cleanly; the highest-impact failures — BOLA, business-flow abuse, fail-open error paths, moderation quality — do not. Phase 3 must resist shrinking skills to what CI can check: every skill above pairs its automatable gates with the manual test or review that catches what the scanner cannot, and drafters should keep that pairing explicit.
- **Overlap management.** Session-security, auth-hardening, and api-security share the authentication surface; dependency-safety and supply-chain share the SBOM; encryption underpins secrets-management. Phase 3 should assign each control exactly one owning skill and cross-reference from the others, or the three auth-adjacent skills will drift into contradicting each other (e.g. different timeout or rate-limit numbers).
